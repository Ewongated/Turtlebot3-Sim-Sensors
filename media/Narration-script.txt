0:00–0:10 — Introduction
This is Project 11. Our simulated TurtleBot moves around obstacles, then docks
at a marked charging station.

0:10–0:48 — Normal run
This is the full normal run at normal speed. The large view shows the robot in
the room, and the smaller view shows what its camera sees.

The robot uses LiDAR to find obstacles and plan a path around them. It doesn't
get its true position from the simulator for control. Instead, the controller
uses the position estimated by the filter.

It finishes this run in 37.3 seconds without a recorded collision.

0:48–1:01 — Docking replay
Here is docking again. Without the marker, the robot stops advancing and turns
to find it. Arrival needs five accepted camera readings.

1:01–1:20 — How the estimate works
This replay is at half speed. The state is the robot's x position, y position and
heading. Wheel encoders estimate how it has moved. The camera corrects drift when
it sees the marker. Q represents motion uncertainty, while R represents uncertainty
in the camera measurements.

1:20–2:06 — Noisy camera run
This full run adds image noise and randomly drops thirty percent of camera
observations. It is playing at normal speed.

The filter keeps predicting movement from the encoders when a camera observation
is missing. When a suitable reading returns, it can correct the estimate.

Below the camera are the estimation errors and uncertainty bands. The numbers
on the left compare the true and estimated position. True position is used here
to evaluate the result, not to steer the robot.

This trial finishes in 45.3 seconds, with no recorded collision.

2:06–2:45 — Prediction and correction replay
This section is replayed at half speed so the changes are easier to follow.
Watch the camera-update label. A camera picture can still be visible even when
its observation has been dropped by the test.

During prediction-only periods, uncertainty grows. When the camera correction
is accepted, the estimate and its uncertainty change.

The camera also needs calibration. A separate checkerboard study checked the
simulated camera settings, but the marker measurements still have some range
bias and occasional large heading errors. The filter is not perfect.

2:45–3:22 — Shifted obstacle and wheel bias
Here the obstacle has moved, and a small difference is added to the wheel
measurements. The planner has to use the new LiDAR readings rather than assuming
the old obstacle position.

The LQR controller follows the planned path using estimated sideways and heading
errors. Wheel compensation helps account for the simulated actuator friction.

The robot completes this run in 36.9 seconds. The final docking check uses the
same tolerances as the other trials.

3:22–3:58 — Slippery floor
This run reduces the floor friction. That changes how commanded wheel movement
turns into actual robot movement.

This is why encoder-only movement can drift and why camera corrections are useful.
The controller still uses the estimated state throughout the run.

It completes in 35.4 seconds without a recorded collision. This is one tested
friction setting, so it doesn't prove the robot will handle every surface.

3:58–4:20 — Results
The full check used two seeds in each of four conditions. All eight runs finished,
with no recorded collisions or timeouts.

The average position RMSE was 7.54 centimetres. That measures the error over the
whole run, rather than just the final docking position. Two runs per condition
are still a small sample.

4:20–4:36 — Tradeoff
A wider turning threshold fixed a slow case, reducing its time from 90.3 to
36.9 seconds. But average estimation error increased from 6.20 to 7.54 centimetres.
It is a speed-versus-accuracy tradeoff, rather than an improvement in everything.
